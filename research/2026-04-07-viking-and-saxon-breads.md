# Research Note: Viking and Saxon Breads

## Question

What should count as a good first representative bread for:

- Viking-age baking,
- and Anglo-Saxon baking,

if the aim is to stay close to the evidence while still producing recipes that
work in a modern home kitchen without an open hearth or clay bread oven?

## Date note

These two traditions are both best understood as `early medieval`, not truly
`pre-medieval`.

- `Viking Age`: conventionally about `793` to `1066 CE`
- `Anglo-Saxon England`: broadly `5th century` to `1066 CE`

They overlap in time, but not in exactly the same food culture.

## Short answer

The strongest first pair is:

1. a `Viking-style barley, oat, and rye hearth bread`, cooked as thick
   flatbreads on the skillet,
2. a `Late-Saxon mixed wheat and rye loaf`, inspired by the surviving Ipswich
   loaves and baked as a modern Dutch-oven boule.

This gives one bread that feels common, coarse, and hearth-based, and another
that feels recognizably English, grain-mixed, and loaf-like.

## Viking direction

The most useful evidence here points toward coarse, flat or low-profile breads
rather than a lofty white loaf.

The Swedish History Museum shows a Viking grave find from Knivberga described as
pieces of a thick, round, flat loaf probably made with `barley and oats` and
baked on a frying pan or bakestone. That is an excellent anchor for a first
modern-kitchen Viking bread.

The broader cereal picture also matters. Ann-Marie Hansson's archaeobotanical
work on early medieval buried plants in east-central Sweden notes that the four
most common cereal species in Viking-period settlement material were `bread
wheat, rye, oats, and hulled barley`. The same paper also notes that Birka
grave buns could be more complex or prestigious products, including emmer and
other wheats, while the Viking Ship Museum's `Birka bread` recipe explicitly
describes its wheat, butter, and honey version as a finer biscuit-like bread.

House conclusion for Viking:

- the most representative first recipe should not be the richer Birka biscuit,
- it should be a coarse hearth bread,
- barley and oats should lead the blend,
- and a moderate amount of rye is justified as a structural helper because rye
  is historically attested in Viking contexts and makes the bread far more
  manageable without becoming obviously modern.

This is an inference from sources, not a claim that one exact archaeologically
recovered loaf had this precise formula.

## Saxon direction

The clearest specific bread evidence is from Ipswich Museum's carbonized loaves
from the Buttermarket excavations. The museum states that analysis done in 1990
found the bread to be a mixture of `rye, emmer wheat, and bread/club wheat`,
and suggests that the closest supermarket equivalent today would be a `rye
sourdough boule`.

That individual loaf evidence sits within a broader archaeobotanical pattern.
Wessex Archaeology notes that early Saxon charred remains consistently include
`bread wheat, barley`, with `some oats and rye`. Their wider bread-history note
also says that after the Roman period, Britain shifted away from spelt toward
`bread wheat, barley, rye, and oats`, and that these cereals were used to
produce different breads depending on means and taste.

House conclusion for Saxon:

- the best first recipe is a mixed-grain loaf rather than a flatbread,
- bread wheat should remain the main structural flour,
- rye should be clearly present,
- and a modern ancient-wheat stand-in is reasonable because the Ipswich loaves
  contained emmer wheat.

Since emmer is not yet in the pantry, `wholemeal spelt` is the best available
stand-in. It is not identical to emmer, but it is a much more historically
honest substitute than using only modern white flour.

## Why these two make a good starting pair

They show a useful contrast:

- the Viking bread expresses coarse grain, flat form, and hearth or pan baking,
- the Saxon bread expresses a more clearly loaf-like mixed-wheat tradition.

That makes them a strong opening move for the repo's early-medieval line of
experiments.

## Suggested next experiments

- Later add a richer `Birka biscuit` or `feast bread` as the higher-status
  Viking companion to the everyday hearth bread.
- Revisit the Saxon loaf when emmer becomes available so the spelt stand-in can
  be retired.
- Explore whether a barm- or sponge-led Saxon version produces a more
  convincing eating quality than the simpler instant-yeast adaptation.

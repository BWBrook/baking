# Research Note: Barley, Buckwheat, and Quinoa Breads

## Question

How should the new ingest notes on barley flour, buckwheat flour, and quinoa
flour be translated into practical house recipes for:

- a savory skillet quick bread for soups and stews,
- and a yeasted Dutch-oven maslin-style loaf?

## Short answer

The two breads should be treated differently. Quick breads tolerate high
alternative-flour usage much better than yeasted loaves, so the skillet bread
can give barley, buckwheat, and quinoa all substantial roles. The yeasted loaf
needs wheat to remain the clear structural majority, with buckwheat and quinoa
held to more conservative supporting percentages.

## Flour behavior summary

### Barley flour

- Bob's Red Mill lists barley flour as workable at up to `50%` substitution in
  white- or wheat-flour baking.
- King Arthur groups barley with the more substitution-tolerant ancient grains.
- Flavor-wise, barley tends to read as mild, sweet, and softening rather than
  aggressive.

### Buckwheat flour

- Bob's Red Mill suggests about `20%` substitution as a broad starting point.
- King Arthur describes buckwheat as bold, toasty, and rich, with a texture
  that stays moist and tender in small amounts but can become chalky in larger
  ones.
- Bob's own buckwheat yeast bread shows that much higher usage is possible, but
  it requires substantial support from other flours and a sponge-like method.

### Quinoa flour

- Bob's Red Mill suggests about `25%` substitution as a broad starting point.
- King Arthur describes quinoa as bold and nutty, but dry in larger amounts.
- Bob's quinoa yeast bread uses roughly `17%` quinoa flour against a larger
  white-flour base, which is a useful practical benchmark.

## Quick bread conclusion

For the skillet quick bread:

- high alternative-flour usage is acceptable,
- quick-bread chemistry is forgiving,
- the bread is meant to be hearty and stew-friendly rather than airy.

That supports a house blend around:

- `60%` barley,
- `20%` buckwheat,
- `20%` quinoa.

Why not the rougher `50/30/20` split from the draft note as the default?

- because keeping buckwheat at `20%` aligns better with the broader guidance,
- while `20%` quinoa still gives clear flavor,
- and the extra barley makes the loaf milder and more dependable as a first
  house version.

Psyllium is worth using here because the flour blend has very little reliable
gluten structure.

## Yeasted maslin conclusion

For the yeasted Dutch-oven loaf:

- wheat must stay dominant,
- buckwheat and quinoa should be supporting flours,
- folds and hydration matter more than heroic kneading.

The strongest house default is roughly:

- `73%` strong white flour,
- `17%` buckwheat,
- `10%` quinoa.

This keeps the total non-wheat share under `30%`, which fits comfortably within
King Arthur's caution that yeast breads become dense and fractured when ancient
grain substitutions rise too high.

## House conclusion

The ingest material naturally becomes two recipes:

1. a skillet stew bread where the alternative flours are the main event,
2. a Dutch-oven maslin where wheat is the support framework and the other
   flours are controlled flavor contributors.

## Suggested next experiments

- Compare the `60/20/20` skillet bread against a more rustic `50/30/20`
  version.
- Trial the maslin with a slightly smaller buckwheat share if the crumb feels
  too tight.
- Consider a later barley-inclusive yeasted loaf once the current buckwheat and
  quinoa maslin is tested.

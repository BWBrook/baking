# Research Note: Georgian French Bread

## Question

How should the new intake notes on `French Bread` be translated into a recipe
that is historically grounded, useful in the current kitchen, and distinct
from both modern baguette-style bread and full brioche?

## Short answer

The strongest interpretation is an enriched white table bread in the English
`French Bread` tradition, not a modern French lean loaf. The historical record
shows a range within that family. Earlier eighteenth-century recipes, such as
Eliza Smith and the MS.7746 manuscript, lean toward soft, lightly handled
milk-and-egg doughs baked in dishes. Late Georgian John Mollard still treats
`French Bread` as rich white bread, but with milk, butter, a little sugar, and
brick loaves baked on tins. For this repo, the best fit is a middle path: a
white-flour, milk-rich, moderately egged and buttered dough, mixed by sponge,
developed only modestly, and baked as two short tray loaves.

## Historical framing

- English `French Bread` in the eighteenth century was a status loaf of fine
  white flour and genteel enrichment.
- It is historically closer to a soft luxury table bread than to a baguette.
- The name likely reflects English ideas of French refinement as much as any
  direct continuity with one specific French loaf.
- The line between enriched bread and cake was softer than modern bakery
  categories imply.

## Source patterns

### Eliza Smith

Eliza Smith's `French Bread` gives the classic early pattern:

- fine white flour,
- eggs,
- ale yeast,
- warm milk,
- a thin light paste,
- explicit instruction not to knead,
- baking in wooden dishes,
- crust removed by rasping after baking.

This is the clearest evidence that some English `French Bread` recipes were
much softer and more delicately handled than ordinary white bread.

### Wellcome MS.7746

The manuscript recipe strongly supports the same broad family:

- flour,
- ale yeast,
- egg whites,
- milk and water,
- dough baked in dishes,
- quick hot oven.

This confirms that the dish-baked, enriched, softly worked style was not an
isolated printed curiosity.

### John Mollard

Mollard's 1802 `French Bread` keeps the same identity but in a more structured
late-Georgian form:

- fine flour,
- sponge first,
- milk as the liquid,
- butter,
- a little sugar,
- nice stiffness,
- knead, prove, knead again,
- mould into bricks and bake on tins.

This is especially useful for the repo because it lands squarely in the
Georgian period and provides a practical bridge between the softer earlier
versions and a modern bakeable house formula.

### Townsends and modern reconstruction context

Townsends is helpful as a reconstruction context rather than a primary source.
Its no-knead version emphasizes what surprises modern bakers most:

- the dough is enriched,
- it can be very soft,
- it can push toward a bread-cake texture,
- Dutch-oven baking is a modern interpretive convenience rather than what the
  original recipes specify.

That helps explain the user's earlier result that felt `almost cake like`.

## Flour choice for this repo

The user's available white flours are:

- standard white flour at about 11% protein,
- high-gluten white flour at about 21% protein.

Best historical choice:

- use the standard white flour as the baseline.

Reason:

- the defining historical feature is refinement, not modern extreme gluten
  strength.
- too much strong flour risks pushing the loaf toward chewiness and away from
  the soft white crumb suggested by the sources.

## Shape and equipment choice

The strongest equipment choice here is not the Dutch oven.

Reason:

- historical recipes point toward dishes or tins,
- the intended loaf seems to favor a softer crust than the Dutch oven tends to
  produce,
- the user's current kitchen does not yet include loaf tins,
- tray-baked `bricks` are closer to Mollard's form than a boule would be.

That makes a lined baking tray the best house adaptation for now.

## Practical implications for this repo

- Keep the flour fully white.
- Let milk be the dominant liquid.
- Include egg and butter, but moderately.
- Avoid fully modern intensive kneading.
- Bake as short tray loaves rather than a single round Dutch-oven loaf.
- Treat a more cake-like or more brioche-like version as a later variation, not
  the baseline.

## Inference notes

These parts are modern choices rather than direct historical instructions:

- using instant dry yeast instead of ale yeast or barm,
- standardizing the batch at 750 g flour,
- reducing the egg level from the richer early versions to keep the loaf more
  bread-like,
- using two tray-baked brick loaves rather than dishes or dedicated tins,
- setting modern fan-forced oven temperatures and timings.

## Suggested next experiments

- Compare this tray-baked version against a covered-skillet dish version.
- Try a more overtly Eliza-Smith-style paste loaf with less kneading and a
  wetter dough.
- Try a more overtly Mollard-style version with less egg and slightly firmer
  handling.
- Trial a small suet variant only as a side experiment, because that seems to
  move the loaf further toward bun or cake territory.

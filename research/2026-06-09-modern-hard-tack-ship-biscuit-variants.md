# Research Note: Modern Hard Tack and Ship's Biscuit Variants

## Question

How should historically inspired hard tack or ship's biscuit be adapted into a
set of practical, more palatable, long-lasting modern biscuits using the
current pantry?

## Short answer

Keep the core historical logic:

- flour,
- water,
- salt,
- stiff dough,
- docking or perforation,
- and thorough drying,

but make the home version thinner, more varied, and less punishing. The best
house direction is a family of crisp, dry, savoury biscuits using different
flour blends, with optional herbs and only a modest amount of oil when eating
quality matters more than maximum storage life.

## Historical baseline

Royal Museums Greenwich describes late 18th-century ship's biscuit as a hard-
baked plain biscuit made from flour and salt, perforated to help baking, and
issued as standard naval food.

The USS Constitution Museum emphasizes that period documents usually called
the ration `biscuit`, `ship's biscuit`, `ship's bread`, or just `bread`, and
that `hardtack` became a popular term later. Their home demonstration recipe
uses whole wheat flour and water only, rolls the dough very thick, punches deep
holes, and bakes very slowly for several hours.

The National Park Service's Gettysburg education recipe gives a simpler Civil
War-style school activity version: flour, water, salt, a half-inch sheet, and
two half-hour bakes at `400 F`, followed by cooling in the closed oven.

## What this means for the repo

The user's goal is not strict naval authenticity. That frees the house recipe
to:

- roll the biscuits thinner,
- use a higher oven temperature than the ultra-authentic version,
- include mixed grains,
- allow optional dry herbs and spices,
- and use a small amount of olive oil for palatability when long storage is
  not the only goal.

The practical tradeoff is clear:

- fat-free biscuits should keep best,
- oil-enriched biscuits should eat better,
- and all versions need to be genuinely dry before storage.

## Flour-blend implications

The current pantry suggests four useful directions:

- a mostly wheat `cabin bread` baseline,
- a rye-spelt maslin version,
- a blue masa and quinoa version,
- and a barley-oat pantry biscuit.

The non-wheat variants need wheat support because the target is a dry, portable
biscuit rather than a crumbly meal cake. Blue masa, quinoa, barley, and oat all
bring flavour, but none should be asked to provide the whole structure in the
first trial.

## Storage and safety

USDA/FSIS shelf-stable guidance is useful at the principle level: shelf-stable
foods rely on heat, drying, and proper packaging, and dry goods should be kept
cool and dry. For these home biscuits, the repo should avoid promising
commercial shelf life or multi-year storage.

House guidance:

- cool fully before storing,
- store only once dry all the way through,
- use an airtight tin or jar,
- keep in a cool, dry cupboard,
- and discard if there is mould, dampness, mustiness, or rancid smell.

## Suggested next experiments

- Bake two half-batches side by side: the cabin bread baseline and the maslin
  rye-spelt version.
- Compare fat-free against `10 g` olive oil per `300 g` flour.
- Record whether the biscuits stay crisp after one week in a jar.
- Decide whether the blue masa/quinoa version needs psyllium support.

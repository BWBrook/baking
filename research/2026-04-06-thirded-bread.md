# Research Note: Thirded Bread

## Question

How should the rough Townsends-inspired `thirded bread` notes in the intake file
be turned into a historically grounded recipe for this baking notebook?

## Short answer

The safest historical framing is to call this an `18th-century-style thirded
bread` or a `colonial-style rye-and-Indian loaf`, not a rigidly authentic
single-source reconstruction. The bread family is unquestionably historical:
Townsends demonstrates a rye-and-Indian loaf using a barm-based sponge, a stiff
dough, and only a restrained single proof; nineteenth-century American sources
also show both scalded Indian-rye breads and formulas using equal proportions
of wheat, rye, and Indian corn. That means the equal-thirds version is a
plausible historical member of the broader lineage, even though exact ratios
varied. For this repo, the best first draft is a 750 g Dutch-oven boule using
250 g each of cornmeal, rye, and strong white wheat flour, a quick sponge,
optional molasses, and one modest rise only.

## Sourced notes

| Source | Key point | Link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Townsends, `Rye and Indian Bread` | Demonstrates a barm-based sponge, a stiff dough, and a rise only to about 50% larger, not doubled; Townsends' own working ratio is 1 cup Indian meal, 1 cup rye flour, and 2 cups wheat flour. | [Rye and Indian Bread](https://www.townsends.us/blogs/blog/rye-indian-bread) |
| Eliza Leslie, *Miss Leslie's New Cookery Book* | States that excellent bread may be made of equal proportions of wheat, rye flour, and Indian corn; also gives Indian-rye and Indian-wheat breads made by pouring scalding liquid over the meal before adding yeast. | [Miss Leslie's New Cookery Book](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/40943/pg40943-images.html) |
| Alice Morse Earle, *Colonial Dames and Good Wives* | Describes `Rhineinjun` or rye-and-Indian bread as large round loaves and records one old formula of one quart rye meal to two quarts cornmeal, baked in basins or in the oven with a cup of water to soften the crust. | [Colonial dames and good wives](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71532.html.images) |
| Zingerman's Roadhouse, `Roadhouse Bread from the Bakehouse` | A modern historical bakery note that describes the same bread family as commonly known in New England as `Rye 'n' Indian` or `Thirded Bread` in the 18th and 19th centuries. | [Roadhouse Bread from the Bakehouse](https://www.zingermansroadhouse.com/2020/08/roadhouse-bread-from-the-bakehouse/) |

## Practical implications for this repo

- It is historically safer to treat `thirded bread` as one variant in the
  larger rye-and-Indian family rather than the only correct formula.
- The Townsends handling notes are especially useful because they match the
  dough physics: stiff mix, no panic-watering, one moderate rise, and acceptance
  of a dense loaf.
- Equal thirds is supportable as a recipe shape because Leslie explicitly notes
  equal proportions of wheat, rye, and Indian corn.
- A round loaf is a good historical fit and also matches the current house
  equipment.
- Strong white flour is a sensible modern choice for the wheaten third because
  it gives the low-gluten mix its best chance of cohesion.

## Inference notes

These parts are modern house choices rather than direct historical dictates:

- using exactly 750 g total flour,
- using instant dry yeast instead of barm,
- baking in the Dutch oven,
- setting the oven temperatures for the fan-forced home oven,
- choosing strong white bread flour over a more rustic or lower-strength wheat
  flour,
- using the equal-thirds form as the notebook's baseline version.

# Research Note: Unleavened Flatbreads with Chickpea, Quinoa, and Buckwheat Flour

## Question

How should the repo develop a trio of true unleavened flatbreads that feel
authentic in character while still fitting the current pantry and home-kitchen
setup?

Desired directions:

- a chickpea flatbread with a Middle Eastern or adjacent feel,
- a quinoa flatbread with an Andean or Inca-like feel,
- and a buckwheat flatbread with a Russian bias.

## Short answer

The strongest route is not to force all three into the same form. Instead:

- build the chickpea bread from the Arab-Andalusian chickpea-pancake and
  socca or farinata family,
- build the quinoa bread from the Uru-Aymara `qarasiña` or `kaswira` family,
- and build the buckwheat bread from the Russian quick-blini family.

That yields three flatbreads that are all genuinely unleavened, but each with
its own cultural logic and texture.

## Chickpea direction

The best documented historical anchor here is the Arab-Andalusian chickpea
pancake. Professor Daniel Newman presents a 13th-century Muslim Spanish recipe
using chickpea flour to make a thin pan-fried cake, with eggs, yeast, fennel,
and spices. That is not identical to the house brief because the user wanted a
true unleavened bread. However, it clearly establishes a historical
chickpea-flatbread thread in the Arabic world.

The most practical modern companion tradition is the western Mediterranean
socca or farinata family: chickpea flour, olive oil, and a hot pan or skillet.
This gives a stable no-yeast method and fits the current cast-iron setup very
well.

House conclusion:

- treat the chickpea bread as a modern unleavened adaptation that sits between
  Arab-Andalusian chickpea pancakes and socca or farinata,
- keep it simple and olive-oil led,
- and use optional warm spices to push the flavor toward the desired regional
  feel.

This is an inference from sources rather than a claim that one exact medieval
Middle Eastern flatbread survives unchanged in the recipe file.

## Quinoa direction

The quinoa bread has the clearest indigenous American anchor of the three.
FAO describes quinoa as originating in the Andes and emphasizes the way Andean
indigenous peoples preserved it through traditional knowledge and practice.
So the right claim here is `Andean` rather than narrowly or confidently
`imperial Inca`.

More specifically, recent heritage research on Uru-Aymara foodways documents
`qarasiña` or `kaswira` as a quinoa-flour food from the Lake Titicaca region.
The paper describes quinoa flour, limewater, salt, water, and oil or fat,
flattened thin and fried in a pan. It also notes that a small amount of wheat
flour may be added to keep the dough from breaking during stretching.

House conclusion:

- the Andean flatbread should be built as a `kaswira`-inspired griddle bread,
- quinoa should remain the dominant flour,
- and a modern support flour is reasonable for handling.

I chose blue masa harina as that support flour. This is an inference from the
sources, not a direct traditional formula. The reason is practical and
cultural: it avoids leaning on wheat, and maize still feels much closer to the
pre-wheat Andean world than white flour does. The result is a modern,
quinoa-centred adaptation rather than a strict reconstruction.

## Buckwheat direction

The Russian anchor is straightforward: buckwheat blini. The Russian UNESCO
Commission notes that buckwheat blini were considered the classic indigenous
Russian form, especially in central Russia. The same source also records a
historical quick, yeast-free blini branch using wheat flour, butter, sour
milk, egg yolks, and whipped whites.

That quick branch is ideal for the current brief because it allows a truly
unleavened bread that still feels recognizably Russian. Saveur's blini recipe,
adapted from Darra Goldstein, reinforces the common pairing of buckwheat with a
supporting wheat flour.

House conclusion:

- the buckwheat recipe should be a quick skillet blini rather than a rolled
  dough flatbread,
- buckwheat should remain the lead flour,
- and a support flour is justified because buckwheat alone is fragile.

I chose wholemeal spelt as the support flour. This is again an inference from
the sources rather than a claim that spelt is the canonical Russian partner
flour. The point is to preserve a rustic grain character while still giving the
blini a manageable structure.

## House conclusion

The ingest request naturally becomes three different flatbread styles:

1. an Arab-Andalusian and socca-inspired chickpea skillet flatbread,
2. a `kaswira`-inspired quinoa and blue-masa griddle bread,
3. and a Russian-style buckwheat and spelt quick blini.

This is better than forcing all three into one generic rolled-flatbread method.

## Suggested next experiments

- Compare the quinoa breads against a version that uses a small amount of
  spelt instead of blue masa for easier handling.
- Decide whether the chickpea flatbread wants a stronger spice identity or a
  plainer olive-oil profile.
- Try a thinner buckwheat blini version specifically for filled or rolled
  serving.

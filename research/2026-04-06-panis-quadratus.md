# Research Note: Panis Quadratus

## Question

How should the `panis quadratus` intake notes be turned into a historically
grounded recipe for this repository?

## Short answer

The safest way to frame a house recipe is as a `Pompeii-style panis quadratus`
or `first-century Pompeii wheat loaf, modern adaptation`. The archaeological
form is secure: a round segmented loaf, divided deeply enough to break into
portions, with a belt-like groove around its side. What is not secure is the
full ingredient formula. Current research argues that the Pompeian loaves were
made with a stiffer dough than many modern reconstructions assume, that the
belt did not necessarily require string, and that the bake was probably shorter
than many modern hobby versions. The strongest grain baseline is wheat, not
rye, with room for later experimental variants using emmer or spelt once the
main wheat version is established.

## What seems firm

- Eighty-one `panis quadratus` loaves were preserved in Pompeii.
- The loaf form is deliberately segmented and was clearly widely recognizable.
- The dough was likely comparatively stiff.
- The exact grain blend remains uncertain.

## Grain context

### Wheat

Bread wheat is the safest default. National Geographic reports that
`Triticum aestivum`, bread wheat, was found alongside panis quadratus loaves in
Pompeii bakery excavations. Rickman's synthesis also argues that by Roman
imperial times `triticum vulgare`, the ancestor of modern bread wheat, had
become a major autumn-sown crop in Campania and Etruria, even while other naked
wheats remained important.

### Emmer and durum

Emmer mattered historically in Rome because early Roman `far` was emmer.
Rickman also emphasizes that naked wheats of the emmer group, especially durum,
were major Mediterranean cereals and important in Egypt and southern Italy. But
that is not the same as demonstrating that a commercial Pompeii loaf such as
panis quadratus was mainly emmer.

### Spelt, einkorn, barley, millet

The broader Vesuvian archaeobotanical record shows that the region knew more
than one cereal. A recent study of the Naples museum collection reports bread
wheat, emmer, einkorn, barley, and millet among the food plants from the area
buried by the eruption. That widens the possible grain world, but it still does
not identify the exact flour stock of panis quadratus itself.

### Practical implication

For a reconstruction recipe, the safest baseline is still a wheat loaf. Emmer,
spelt, or other grains are better treated as later experimental branches rather
than the first default version.

## Shape and process context

- The 2023 interdisciplinary review argues that the horizontal belt did not
  necessarily require string.
- The same review argues for a shorter bake than many modern reconstructions.
- A segmented, compact, shape-holding loaf matters more than chasing open crumb.

## Leavening context

Roman bakers certainly used leavens. National Geographic notes that Roman
starters could include adjuncts such as legumes or grape skins. For this repo,
the practical stand-in is a quick sponge with instant yeast. That preserves the
preferment logic without forcing a speculative ancient-starter recreation as the
baseline recipe.

## Practical implications for this repo

- Keep the baseline loaf wheat-based.
- Keep hydration distinctly lower than a modern artisan boule.
- Treat the belt groove as optional in the first practical version.
- Prefer a preheated baking tray over the Dutch oven for the baseline recipe,
  because the shape and exterior markings matter more here than enclosed steam.
- Keep rye, barley, and millet out of the base formula unless deliberately
  testing alternative Roman or lower-status grain profiles later.

## Inference notes

These parts are modern house choices rather than secure archaeological facts:

- using 750 g total flour,
- using strong white flour plus a little wholemeal wheat as a stand-in for a
  coarser Roman flour stock,
- using instant dry yeast in a sponge,
- baking on a preheated tray in a fan-forced home oven,
- leaving out speculative herbs and seeds in the baseline version.

## Suggested next experiments

- Compare the baseline loaf against an all-white wheat version.
- Try a 10% to 15% emmer variant once emmer is acquired.
- Test a version with a more pronounced equatorial groove.
- Try a barm-like or sourdough-like starter later if the wheat baseline proves
  satisfying.

## Extra contextual lead

Farrell Monaco's published and interview material, and Max Miller's `Tasting
History` episode on the loaf, remain useful for practical reconstruction
context, but they should be treated as interpretive modern baking guides rather
than direct proof of the exact Pompeii formula.

# Research Note: Georgian Fine White Boule

## Question

How should the intake notes for a simple white Dutch-oven boule be translated
into a recipe that reads as an upper-class Georgian household loaf rather than
just a generic modern white boule?

## Short answer

The strongest route is a `fine white table bread` interpretation rather than a
plain baker's white loaf. Georgian white bread had status value because fine
white flour still required more careful sifting and refinement. John Mollard's
1802 bread recipes give two especially useful models: `English Bread` made from
the best white flour with a sponge and repeated proving, and richer `French
Bread` made from fine flour with milk, butter, and a little sugar. For this
repo, the best fit is a mostly simple white boule using standard white flour, a
light sponge, a little milk and butter, and a second handling before final
shaping. That keeps it recognizably Georgian and upper-table without drifting
into brioche territory.

## Historical framing

- The Federation of Bakers notes that in Georgian times new Chinese silk sieves
  helped produce finer, whiter flour and that white bread remained associated
  with upper-class privilege for a long time.
- The 1709 bread regime distinguished white, wheaten, and household bread,
  which reflects the social meaning of flour refinement.
- Mollard's book is directly relevant because it was written for the tables of
  noblemen, gentlemen, and tradesmen and includes both plain and richer white
  breads.

## Source patterns

### Mollard's English Bread

This gives the structural model:

- best white flour,
- sponge or leaven first,
- firm dough,
- proof,
- knead again,
- proof again,
- brisk oven.

That repeated working fits the disciplined, well-made white loaf better than a
modern no-knead or ultra-wet dough.

### Mollard's French Bread

This gives the refinement model:

- fine flour,
- milk,
- butter,
- a little loaf sugar,
- nice stiffness,
- careful proving.

This is especially useful for imagining an upper-table bread meant to eat well
when fresh.

### Acton's Bordyke Bread and household guidance

Acton's bread notes reinforce several points that are still useful:

- the leaven should be taken at its right moment,
- the dough should be firm and smooth, not too lithe,
- milk was an accepted bread liquid,
- refined breads could be shaped into loaves or baked in round pans.

## Flour choice for this repo

The user's available white flours are:

- standard white flour at about 11% protein,
- high-gluten white flour at about 21% protein.

Best historical choice:

- use the standard white flour as the baseline.

Reason:

- Georgian fine white bread depended on refinement and bolting, not on modern
  ultra-strong industrial gluten levels.
- The 21% flour is useful as a modern structural aid, but it would shift the
  loaf toward a stronger, chewier result than the historical spirit really
  calls for.

## Practical implications for this repo

- Keep the loaf white-flour-dominant.
- Use a sponge.
- Keep the dough firm enough to shape cleanly.
- Use a little milk and butter, but only lightly.
- Keep the boule shape as a house adaptation, not as a claim of exact period
  form.

## Inference notes

These parts are modern choices rather than direct historical instructions:

- using the Dutch oven,
- choosing boule shape over brick or pan loaf shape,
- using instant dry yeast,
- setting the formula at 750 g flour for the house version,
- preferring standard 11% flour over the 21% flour.

## Suggested next experiments

- Compare this loaf against a plainer all-water English-bread version.
- Compare it against a richer Mollard-inspired French-bread adaptation.
- Try a version using 10% to 15% of the stronger flour for shape support and
  compare crumb texture.

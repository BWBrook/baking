# Scalds

This note records the main scald-style techniques that matter for this
repository, especially for softer breads, wholegrain handling, and historical
or European-style baking.

## What a scald is

In bread work, a scald is a pre-treatment in which part of the flour, meal, or
grain is mixed with hot or boiling liquid, or cooked into a paste, before being
added to the final dough.

The main effects are:

- improved water absorption,
- softer crumb,
- slower staling,
- gentler handling of wholegrain or coarse material,
- in some styles, a sweeter or fuller grain flavour.

Strictly speaking, not every related pre-soak is a scald. Some are cold or warm
soakers rather than true scalds.

## Why use a scald

Scalds are most useful when one or more of these are true:

- the flour is wholegrain, coarse, or thirsty,
- the loaf should stay soft for longer,
- the dough includes seeds, flakes, or chops,
- the grain is rye-heavy and benefits from pre-hydration,
- the target texture is tender rather than dry and crumbly.

## Main types

## Yudane

Yudane is a Japanese scald in which boiling water is poured over flour and
mixed, usually at about a 1:1 flour-to-liquid ratio by weight.

What it tends to do:

- increases moisture retention,
- improves softness,
- gives a slightly chewier and more resilient crumb than tangzhong,
- works well in enriched or sandwich-style breads.

Typical workflow:

1. Mix flour with boiling liquid.
2. Let it cool fully.
3. Add it to the final dough.

## Tangzhong

Tangzhong is a cooked flour paste, usually made by heating flour and liquid
together until the mixture thickens into a starch gel.

Typical pattern:

- around 1:5 flour-to-liquid by weight,
- cooked until it becomes an opaque paste,
- then cooled before mixing into the dough.

What it tends to do:

- increases dough hydration capacity,
- makes crumb softer and more pillowy,
- slows staling noticeably,
- works especially well in milk breads, sandwich loaves, buns, and soft rolls.

For practical purposes in this repo, tangzhong is the most useful scald when
the goal is soft texture plus better keeping quality.

## European variants

German-speaking baking traditions often distinguish several related
unfermented pre-doughs.

## Brühstück

Brühstück is the closest straightforward European scald: flour, meal, seeds, or
 flakes are mixed with boiling or very hot water and then left to cool before
going into the final dough.

Best uses:

- wholegrain breads,
- seeded breads,
- rye-heavy doughs,
- breads where freshness and moisture matter.

In practical terms, this is often the easiest European-style scald to borrow
into home baking.

## Kochstück

Kochstück is a cooked portion or cooked paste. In practice it is the European
variant most similar to tangzhong: flour or other starchy material is cooked
with water into a paste before being added to the dough.

Best uses:

- softer wheat breads,
- wholegrain breads that need better freshness,
- breads using old bread, crumbs, or other starchy adjuncts,
- cases where you want a moist crumb without pushing final-dough hydration too
  far.

If you want a European-flavoured way to get some of the tangzhong effect,
Kochstück is the nearest cousin.

## Quellstück

Quellstück is best thought of as a nearby cousin rather than a true scald. It
is a soak or swelling piece, usually done cold or warm rather than by cooking.

Best uses:

- seeds and cracked grains,
- bran-heavy doughs,
- formulas where inclusions need time to absorb water before mixing.

Useful distinction:

- `Quellstück` = soak
- `Brühstück` = hot-water scald
- `Kochstück` = cooked paste

## Rye-specific scalds

Rye deserves special mention because hot-water scalds are especially useful in
rye work.

Why:

- rye binds water strongly,
- rye dough becomes sticky and pasty much more quickly than wheat dough,
- and rye-heavy breads often benefit from a pre-hydrated, partially gelatinized
  rye portion.

What a rye scald tends to do:

- pre-hydrates thirsty rye flour, meal, or chops,
- gelatinizes part of the starch,
- improves moisture retention and sliceability,
- and gives a fuller, rounder rye flavour.

One related European term worth knowing is `AromaStück`, a thermally guided
warm scald held long enough to develop extra aroma and natural maltose.

Important limit:

- a hot rye scald is not the same thing as acidifying rye,
- and at high rye percentages a sourdough or other acidic rye phase still does
  stabilizing work that a straight hot scald does not replace.

## Historical and style notes

- Rye and northern European breads often benefit from some form of scald or hot
  soak.
- In modern Asian baking, tangzhong and yudane are common routes to soft,
  fresh-keeping crumb.
- These methods are related in function even when the cultural labels differ.

## House guidance

For this repo, the most likely uses are:

- `Tangzhong` for soft sandwich loaves, buns, or milk-bread-adjacent work.
- `Yudane` when a simpler scalded-flour method is wanted without cooking a
  paste on the stove.
- `Brühstück` for wholegrain, rye, seed, or historical breads where coarse
  material needs pre-hydration.
- `Kochstück` when a European-style cooked paste would suit the recipe better
  than calling it tangzhong.

# Retard and Cold Proofing

This note records the practical meaning of `retard` in bread baking and how it
fits the methods used in this repository.

## What `retard` means

In bread baking, to `retard` a dough means to slow fermentation by cooling it,
usually in a refrigerator or dedicated retarder.

This is not a separate leavening system. It is still yeast- or sourdough-driven
fermentation, just happening much more slowly.

In home baking language, `retard`, `cold retard`, and `cold proof` are often
used almost interchangeably. The most common meaning is:

- shape the loaf,
- refrigerate it overnight,
- and bake it the next day.

## Why bakers use it

Main benefits:

- a more flexible baking schedule,
- deeper fermentation flavour,
- firmer dough that is easier to score cleanly,
- and, in some doughs, better overall handling.

King Arthur explicitly notes that chilling shaped loaves overnight improves
flavour, firms up very wet dough, makes scoring easier, and gives the baker
more scheduling flexibility.

## Two main forms

## Bulk retard

Bulk retard means chilling the dough before dividing or final shaping.

Typical uses:

- make-ahead doughs,
- no-knead or low-knead doughs,
- doughs that need flexibility in timing,
- some enriched doughs.

King Arthur's no-knead dough examples show dough being mixed, given some room-
temperature rise, then refrigerated for later shaping and baking. Their brioche
formula also notes that the dough can be retarded overnight, either after a
bench rest and fold or as soon as mixing is complete.

What bulk retard tends to do:

- spreads the work across two days,
- builds flavour over time,
- and can make rich doughs easier to handle once fully chilled.

## Final retard or cold proof

Final retard means chilling the dough after shaping.

This is the most relevant form for this repo's Dutch-oven boule workflow.

Typical uses:

- banneton-proofed hearth loaves,
- sourdoughs,
- wet or floppy doughs that benefit from firming up,
- and recipes where the loaf should be ready to bake first thing next day.

King Arthur's sourdough examples show shaped boules being placed in a covered
proofing basket in the refrigerator overnight, then baked directly or nearly
directly from cold the next day.

What final retard tends to do:

- helps the loaf keep its shape,
- makes scoring cleaner,
- and lets the bake happen on a controlled schedule.

## What it does not guarantee

Cold proofing is useful, but it is not magic.

Important cautions:

- it does not automatically improve every loaf,
- it does not guarantee a more sour bread,
- and it can reduce volume if the dough goes cold before enough fermentation
  has happened.

King Arthur notes in one sourdough flavour article that refrigeration may add
depth of flavour, but does not guarantee extra sourness. They also warn more
generally that dough set to rise in a cold environment can produce poor volume
and poor structure.

So the practical lesson is:

- do not simply bolt an overnight fridge stay onto every recipe,
- and do not treat the refrigerator as a substitute for adequate fermentation.

## Practical signs and handling notes

- Expect a retarded loaf to feel firmer than a room-temperature loaf.
- Do not expect dramatic doubling in the refrigerator, especially with shaped
  loaves.
- For banneton loaves, a liner or cloth can help when proofing overnight in the
  fridge.
- Cover shaped dough well so the surface does not dry out excessively.

King Arthur's fig-and-walnut sourdough example is especially useful here: the
covered basket goes into the refrigerator overnight, and the loaf may look only
moderately puffed the next morning rather than dramatically expanded.

## House guidance

For this repo, the most likely and most useful application is:

- shaped boule in banneton,
- covered and refrigerated overnight,
- then scored and baked in the Dutch oven the next day.

Good starting situations:

- high-hydration wheat loaves,
- sourdough or levain loaves later on,
- mixed-flour hearth loaves that are otherwise awkward to score,
- and make-ahead dough schedules.

Use more caution with:

- weak low-gluten doughs,
- heavily alternative-flour loaves,
- and recipes not originally designed for a cold proof.

In practical house terms:

- `final retard` is the default meaning unless a recipe says `bulk retard`,
- overnight is the normal first trial,
- and room-temperature fermentation before chilling still matters.

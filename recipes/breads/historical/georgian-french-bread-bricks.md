# Georgian French Bread Bricks

## Goal

A refined enriched white bread in the English `French Bread` tradition of the
Georgian period: soft-crumbed, milk-rich, lightly egged and buttered, and more
genteel than ordinary household bread. This version is a tray-baked modern
adaptation shaped into two short bricks rather than a modern baguette or a
Dutch-oven boule.

## Historical basis

- Eighteenth-century English and Anglo-American `French Bread` was not a lean
  baguette-style loaf. It was generally made from very fine white flour and
  enriched with milk, and often eggs or butter as well.
- Eliza Smith's `French Bread` uses fine flour, eggs, ale yeast, warm milk,
  almost no kneading, and wooden dishes, then calls for the loaves to be
  `rasped` after baking.
- An early eighteenth-century manuscript recipe in Wellcome MS.7746 similarly
  uses flour, egg whites, ale yeast, milk and water, and directs the dough to
  be baked in dishes.
- By 1802, John Mollard's `French Bread` is still rich white bread, but it is
  expressed as a sponge-and-dough method with milk, butter, a little sugar,
  and brick-shaped loaves baked on tins.

See `research/2026-04-06-georgian-french-bread.md` for the source note behind
this recipe.

## Modern adaptation choices

- Uses standard white flour as the default, because it is closer to the spirit
  of fine bolted white flour than the 21% high-gluten flour.
- Uses all milk as the main liquid to keep the loaf clearly in the enriched
  `French Bread` family.
- Uses eggs and butter, but at a moderate level, so the result stays on the
  bread side of the bread-cake boundary.
- Uses a sponge for flavor and structure, then only moderate kneading rather
  than a long modern intensive mix.
- Shapes the dough into two short tray-baked bricks, because that is closer to
  the period `lay them on tins` logic than a Dutch-oven round loaf.
- Uses the house-standard 750 g total flour for a yeasted historical bread
  trial, but splits that into two loaves.

## Method

1. Make the sponge. Mix the sponge flour, milk, and yeast into a thick batter.
   Cover and leave 30 to 45 minutes, until clearly active and risen.
2. In a separate bowl whisk the eggs into the remaining milk. Melt the butter
   and let it cool until just warm, not hot.
3. In a large bowl combine the remaining flour, salt, and sugar.
4. Add the sponge, the milk-and-egg mixture, and the butter to the dry
   ingredients. Mix until all the flour is hydrated. The dough should be soft
   and somewhat sticky, but not pourable.
5. Knead gently for about 4 to 6 minutes, just until the dough becomes more
   cohesive and smoother. Do not try to force it into a dry, tight modern loaf
   dough. If it feels unexpectedly stiff, add 10 g to 20 g extra milk.
6. Cover and leave to bulk ferment until well risen and puffy, usually 60 to
   90 minutes.
7. Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured bench and divide it into two equal
   pieces.
8. Pre-shape each piece into a short round or stubby oval. Rest 10 minutes.
9. Shape each piece into a short brick or thick batard about 16 to 18 cm long.
   Place them seam-side down on a lined or lightly greased baking tray, leaving
   space between them.
10. Cover lightly and final-proof for 30 to 45 minutes, until visibly puffy and
    springy.
11. Preheat the oven to 220 C fan-forced.
12. Bake on the middle shelf at 210 C fan-forced for 22 to 28 minutes, turning
    the tray once if needed, until the loaves are a deep gold and the internal
    temperature is about 93 to 95 C.
13. If you want a softer crust, brush lightly with a little melted butter or
    warm milk after baking and cool under a clean cloth for the first 10 to 15
    minutes.
14. Cool fully before slicing.

## Baking setup

- Total flour target: 750 g
- Yield: 2 enriched brick loaves
- Oven style: electric fan-forced oven
- Default shelf: middle shelf
- Vessel: lined or lightly greased baking tray
- Preheat: 220 C fan-forced
- Bake temperatures: 210 C fan-forced throughout
- Covered or uncovered phases: uncovered bake

## Result targets

- Crumb: soft, fine, and even, but still clearly bread rather than cake
- Crust: thin, golden, and comparatively tender
- Flavor: milky, wheaten, lightly rich, and only faintly sweet
- Shape: two short table loaves or `bricks`, not long baguettes

## Variables to watch

- Whether your flour prefers the extra 10 g to 20 g milk
- Whether the dough is better with slightly less shaping pressure
- Whether a shorter bake gives a softer crust without underbaking the crumb
- Whether the loaves want a little more spacing on the tray

## Flour note

The 21% high-gluten white flour is not the best historical baseline for this
recipe. If you want a little more modern structure, you could replace up to
100 g of the standard flour with the stronger flour, but the default version
stays with the standard white.

## Historical serving note

- This loaf sits in the same family as the refined white breads used for
  genteel tables and for dishes that wanted a soft white crumb.
- If you want to explore period usage later, this is a good candidate for
  toast, bread-and-cream dishes, or recipes that call for crust-less French
  bread.

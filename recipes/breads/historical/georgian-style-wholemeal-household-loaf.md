# Georgian-Style Wholemeal Household Loaf

## Goal

A plain wholemeal loaf inspired by Georgian household-bread practice, but
adapted for a modern home kitchen, instant yeast, and a Dutch oven. This is
not a strict reconstruction. It is a practical first trial for this repository.

## Historical basis

- Georgian bread culture distinguished white, wheaten, and household breads.
- Household bread was associated with coarser and lower-grade flour than white
  bread.
- Early nineteenth-century English bread methods commonly used a sponge, warm
  proving, a second kneading, and a brisk oven.
- Later traditional wholemeal guidance still treated full-meal wheat bread as a
  serious, wholesome loaf and emphasized getting it properly light before
  baking.

See `research/2026-04-06-georgian-household-wholemeal-loaf.md` for the source
note behind this recipe.

## Modern adaptation choices

- Uses 100% plain wholemeal bread wheat flour rather than a mixed household
  flour.
- Uses instant dry yeast instead of ale yeast or brewery yeast.
- Uses a manageable 750 g total flour, which matches the house default for a
  yeasted Dutch-oven loaf.
- Uses a moderate-high hydration and gentle fold-based development, which is an
  inference from wholemeal handling rather than a direct period instruction.
- Bakes as one round hearth loaf in the Dutch oven rather than in a brick oven
  or tin.

## Method

1. Make the sponge. Mix the sponge flour, water, and yeast into a thick paste.
   Cover and leave in a warm room for 30 to 45 minutes, until lightly domed
   and clearly active.
2. Add the remaining flour, 435 g of the remaining water, the salt, and the
   remaining yeast. Mix until no dry flour remains. If the dough feels
   noticeably stiff, add some or all of the final 15 g water.
3. Cover and rest for 20 to 30 minutes so the wholemeal can hydrate.
4. Develop the dough gently. Either knead briefly in the bowl for 3 to 4
   minutes or give the dough three sets of folds over the first hour of bulk
   fermentation. Aim for cohesion, not a highly elastic white-dough feel.
5. Leave to bulk ferment until the dough is visibly fuller and slightly lively,
   usually 60 to 90 minutes in a mild kitchen. Do not wait for dramatic
   doubling if the dough is already aerated and softening.
6. Turn the dough onto a lightly floured bench, pre-shape into a round, and let
   it rest 15 minutes.
7. Shape into a firm boule. Dust the banneton with rice flour and place the
   loaf seam-side up.
8. Final-proof for 45 to 75 minutes, until the dough springs back slowly when
   pressed with a floured finger.
9. Preheat the oven and Dutch oven to 250 C for 45 minutes.
10. Turn the loaf onto baking paper or directly onto a loading surface, score a
    simple cross or one long slash, and load it into the Dutch oven.
11. Bake covered for 20 minutes at 240 C.
12. Remove the lid, reduce to 220 C, and bake for a further 20 to 25 minutes,
    until the loaf is deep brown and the internal temperature is about 96 C.
13. Cool fully before slicing.

## Baking setup

- Total flour target: 750 g
- Oven style: electric fan-forced oven
- Vessel: preheated Dutch oven
- Preheat: 250 C for 45 minutes
- Bake temperatures: 240 C covered, then 220 C uncovered
- Covered or uncovered phases: 20 minutes covered, 20 to 25 minutes uncovered

## Result targets

- Crumb: even, moderately close, but not heavy or gummy
- Crust: well-coloured and firm rather than thin and pale
- Flavor: wheaty, nutty, slightly sweet from the wholemeal
- Shape: round hearth loaf with controlled bloom rather than maximal loft

## Variables to watch

- Whether 78% hydration is enough for this specific wholemeal flour
- Whether the dough benefits from the full 80% hydration
- Whether the final proof is shorter or longer in the fan-forced kitchen setup
- Whether the Dutch oven gives enough oven spring for a fully wholemeal dough

## Next planned changes

- If the loaf bakes up too dense, try either a slightly longer final proof or a
  small hydration increase.
- If the loaf spreads too much, hold the water at the lower end and tighten the
  final shape.
- A later comparison loaf could use 10% to 20% strong white flour as structural
  support, but this first version stays plain wholemeal.

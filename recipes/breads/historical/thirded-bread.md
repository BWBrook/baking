# 18th-Century-Style Thirded Bread

## Goal

A dense, practical historical-style loaf in the rye-and-Indian bread family,
adapted as a 750 g total-flour boule for the house Dutch oven. This version
keeps the equal-thirds idea of wheat, rye, and cornmeal while preserving the
stiff dough, single restrained rise, and barm-like sponge logic from the
Townsends-style approach.

## Historical basis

- New England breads in this family were commonly known as `rye and Indian`
  bread, and modern historical bakers also connect them to `thirded bread`.
- Historical formulas varied. Some older recipes used mostly cornmeal and rye
  with little or no wheat, while later recipes also describe equal proportions
  of wheat, rye, and Indian corn.
- A short sponge based on yeast or barm fits the historical workflow well.
- Some historical versions scalded the Indian meal before mixing, while this
  house version follows the unscalded stiff-dough Townsends-style approach.
- Many historical versions were baked as substantial round loaves rather than
  airy pan breads.

See `research/2026-04-06-thirded-bread.md` for the source note behind this
recipe.

## Modern adaptation choices

- Uses equal thirds by weight: 250 g each of cornmeal, rye flour, and wheat
  flour.
- Uses strong white bread flour for the wheaten third to give the loaf the best
  chance of holding together cleanly.
- Uses instant dry yeast as a modern stand-in for barm.
- Uses a quick sponge to wake the yeast and echo older preferment logic.
- Leaves the cornmeal unscalded, because that fits the Townsends handling you
  collected and helps avoid an overly pasty loaf.
- Uses the house Dutch-oven workflow and fan-forced oven settings.
- Keeps the dough stiff and shape-holding, with only one modest proof.

| --- | --- |

## Method

1. Make the sponge. Mix the sponge flour, water, and yeast into a loose batter.
   Leave for 15 to 25 minutes, until clearly active and foamy.
2. Mix the dry ingredients. In a large bowl, combine the cornmeal, rye flour,
   remaining wheat flour, and salt.
3. Add the molasses to the sponge and stir to dissolve it.
4. Bring the dough together. Pour the sponge mixture into the dry ingredients
   and add 210 g of the remaining water. Mix thoroughly. Do not rush to add
   more water: the dough should be stiff, heavy, and somewhat reluctant.
5. Rest the dough for 20 to 30 minutes so the cornmeal and rye can absorb
   moisture. After the rest, judge whether the final 20 g water is needed.
6. Knead for about 8 to 10 minutes. Do not chase a smooth, stretchy white-loaf
   texture. The target is a cohesive, firm dough that can hold a round shape.
7. Shape into a tight ball and place seam-side up in a well-floured banneton or
   a towel-lined bowl dusted with rye flour and cornmeal.
8. Give one rise only. Proof until the dough is roughly 50% larger and
   noticeably lighter to the touch, not doubled. This will usually take about
   45 to 75 minutes depending on room temperature.
9. Preheat the oven and Dutch oven to 240 C for 45 minutes.
10. Turn the loaf out carefully. Dust the top with cornmeal if desired. A score
    is optional; if used, keep it simple and shallow because this dough does
    not have high tension.
11. Load into the Dutch oven and bake covered for 20 minutes at 230 C.
12. Remove the lid, reduce to 205 C, and bake for a further 30 to 35 minutes,
    until the crust is well coloured and the internal temperature reaches about
    96 to 98 C.
13. Cool fully before slicing. For this loaf, 2 hours is better than 1.

## Baking setup

- Total flour target: 750 g
- Oven style: electric fan-forced oven
- Default shelf: middle shelf
- Vessel: preheated Dutch oven
- Preheat: 240 C for 45 minutes
- Bake temperatures: 230 C covered, then 205 C uncovered
- Covered or uncovered phases: 20 minutes covered, 30 to 35 minutes uncovered

## Result targets

- Crumb: tight, even, and sliceable rather than open
- Crust: firm and well browned, especially if molasses is used
- Flavor: earthy rye, sweet corn, and a dark note from the molasses
- Shape: a compact but respectable boule, not a lofty white-bread dome

## Variables to watch

- Whether the cornmeal on hand wants the full water amount
- Whether the loaf is better without the final held-back water
- Whether the molasses level should be reduced or increased to taste
- Whether a shallower or unscored top gives better structure
- Whether a shorter final proof produces a stronger final shape

## Historical notes for serving

- This bread belongs more to the sturdy, sustaining end of the table than to
  the refined white-loaf tradition.
- Thin slices, especially toasted, are likely to suit the loaf better than
  thick soft slices.
- Butter, drippings, cheese, or extra molasses are all historically plausible
  partners.

## Next planned changes

- If the loaf is too brick-like, try either a small hydration increase or a
  modest increase in the wheat third.
- If the loaf spreads, reduce the final water and proof slightly less.
- A later comparison version could use wholemeal wheat in the wheaten third for
  a more austere loaf, but this first version prioritizes structural success.

# Georgian Fine White Table Boule

## Goal

A fine white boule inspired by the sort of refined white bread that would suit
an upper-class Georgian table: light, well-kneaded, wheat-forward, and made
from carefully sifted flour rather than household meal. This is a modern Dutch-
oven adaptation of that idea, not a strict period reconstruction.

## Historical basis

- By the Georgian period, improved sieving helped produce finer and whiter
  flour, and white bread remained strongly associated with status and quality.
- John Mollard's *The Art of Cookery Made Easy and Refined* gives both `English
  Bread` made from the best white flour and a richer `French Bread` made from
  fine flour, milk, butter, and a little sugar.
- Eliza Acton's bread guidance emphasizes a firm, smooth dough, a proper
  leaven or sponge, and careful repeated handling rather than slack modern dough.
- For a manor-house interpretation, a fine white table loaf is more plausible
  than a rough country boule.

See `research/2026-04-06-georgian-fine-white-boule.md` for the source note
behind this recipe.

## Modern adaptation choices

- Uses standard white flour as the baseline because it is closer in spirit to a
  fine bolted household white flour than the very high-gluten 21% flour.
- Uses a small milk-butter enrichment to lean toward the `French Bread` /
  upper-table style rather than the plainer household loaf.
- Uses instant dry yeast as a stand-in for beer yeast or barm.
- Uses a sponge and a second handling of the dough to keep some Georgian method
  logic in the workflow.
- Uses the house-standard 750 g total flour for a yeasted Dutch-oven loaf.
- Bakes as a Dutch-oven boule because that suits the house setup, even though
  the historical breads were baked in brick or iron ovens.

## Method

1. Make the sponge. Mix the sponge flour, water, milk, and yeast into a thick
   batter. Cover and leave for 30 to 45 minutes, until clearly risen and
   lively.
2. In a large bowl combine the remaining flour, salt, and sugar.
3. Add the sponge, the remaining water and milk, and the softened butter.
4. Mix into a firm dough. It should be smoothable and kneadable, not wet and
   slack.
5. Knead thoroughly for 10 to 12 minutes until the dough is smooth, elastic,
   and capable of a good thin stretch.
6. Cover and let rise until well expanded, usually 60 to 90 minutes depending
   on room temperature.
7. Tip the dough out and knead or fold it briefly again for 1 to 2 minutes.
   This second handling echoes the repeated working described in Georgian bread
   methods.
8. Rest 10 to 15 minutes.
9. Shape into a taut boule and place seam-side up in a floured banneton.
10. Final-proof until the dough springs back slowly when touched, usually 45 to
    75 minutes.
11. Preheat the Dutch oven and lid in the oven at 240 C fan-forced for 45
    minutes.
12. Turn the loaf out, score with one confident slash or a simple cross, and
    load into the Dutch oven.
13. Bake covered for 20 minutes at 230 C fan-forced.
14. Remove the lid, reduce to 210 C fan-forced, and bake for a further 20 to
    25 minutes until the crust is deeply golden and the internal temperature is
    about 96 C.
15. Cool fully before slicing.

## Baking setup

- Total flour target: 750 g
- Oven style: electric fan-forced oven
- Default shelf: middle shelf
- Vessel: preheated Dutch oven
- Preheat: 240 C fan-forced for 45 minutes
- Bake temperatures: 230 C covered, then 210 C uncovered
- Covered or uncovered phases: 20 minutes covered, 20 to 25 minutes uncovered

## Result targets

- Crumb: fine to medium, even, and elegant rather than rustic
- Crust: golden, thin-crisp, and well coloured
- Flavor: clean white-bread sweetness with a faint dairy richness
- Shape: smooth domed boule with controlled bloom

## Variables to watch

- Whether the dough prefers an extra 10 g to 15 g water with your white flour
- Whether a slightly longer final proof opens the crumb more evenly
- Whether the butter level should be reduced for a plainer table-bread style
- Whether a shallower score gives a neater Georgian look

## Flour note

The 21% high-gluten white flour is not the best baseline for this recipe's
historical feel. If you want a little more modern oven spring, you could
replace up to 100 g of the standard flour with the stronger flour, but the
default version stays with the standard white.

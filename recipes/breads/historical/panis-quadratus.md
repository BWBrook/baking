# Pompeii-Style Panis Quadratus

## Goal

A practical home-baking reconstruction of `panis quadratus`, the famous
segmented Pompeian loaf, adapted to the house scale of 750 g total flour. The
aim is a firm, shareable wheat loaf with deep radial divisions, compact crumb,
and a shape that evokes the archaeological loaves without pretending to be a
definitive reconstruction.

## Historical basis

- `Panis quadratus` is the best-known Roman bread form in the archaeological
  record because eighty-one loaves were preserved in Pompeii.
- Current interdisciplinary research argues that the Pompeian loaves were made
  with a stiffer dough than many modern reconstructions assume, that a string
  was not necessarily required to make the equatorial groove, and that the bake
  may have been shorter than many modern recreations.
- Bread wheat or common wheat is the safest modern grain baseline for a Pompeii
  loaf, even though other cereals were also present in the Vesuvian world.
- The precise cereal mix, flour grade, and leaven type of the archaeological
  loaves remain uncertain.

See `research/2026-04-06-panis-quadratus.md` for the source note behind this
recipe.

## Modern adaptation choices

- Uses a wheat-based flour bill rather than a mixed rye or barley loaf.
- Uses 600 g strong white bread flour plus 150 g wholemeal wheat flour as a
  practical stand-in for a somewhat coarser Roman flour stock.
- Uses a quick sponge with instant yeast as a house-friendly stand-in for Roman
  leavening practices.
- Keeps hydration markedly lower than many modern artisan loaves so the loaf
  will hold its disc-like shape and deep segment marks.
- Uses a preheated baking tray rather than the Dutch oven because the form of
  the loaf matters more here than maximizing steam.
- Treats the horizontal belt as optional and secondary to the overall structure.

## Method

1. Make the sponge. Mix the sponge flour, water, and yeast into a thick batter.
   Cover and leave for 20 to 30 minutes, until active and clearly lively.
2. Mix the final dough. Add the remaining flour and salt to a large bowl. Pour
   in the sponge and 250 g of the remaining water. Mix into a firm dough. Only
   add the final 15 g water if the dough refuses to come together.
3. Rest for 15 to 20 minutes so the flour can absorb the water.
4. Knead for about 8 to 10 minutes until the dough is cohesive and moderately
   elastic. Do not chase a soft, highly extensible modern crumb.
5. Bulk ferment until the dough is slightly fuller and smoother, usually 60 to
   90 minutes. This is not a loaf that wants an exaggerated rise.
6. Shape into a tight round and rest 10 minutes.
7. Re-shape into a compact boule, then gently flatten into a thick disc about
   18 to 20 cm across.
8. Place the loaf seam-side down on a sheet of baking paper or a well-floured
   peel.
9. Mark the loaf into eight wedges by making four deep lines through the
   centre: first a cross, then a second cross on the diagonal. Go deep enough
   that the marks survive oven spring.
10. If you want the equatorial belt, press a shallow groove around the side of
    the loaf with a blunt tool or the edge of your hand. Do not let this become
    more important than the overall loaf form.
11. Proof only until slightly puffy, usually 30 to 45 minutes. The loaf should
    look alive, not swollen.
12. Preheat the oven with a heavy baking tray inside to 240 C for 45 minutes.
13. Transfer the loaf onto the hot tray. If desired, add a modest steam pan for
    the first 8 to 10 minutes, but heavy steaming is not required.
14. Bake at 230 C for 15 minutes.
15. Reduce to 210 C and bake for a further 18 to 25 minutes, until the loaf is
    well coloured and the internal temperature reaches about 96 C.
16. Cool fully before breaking or slicing.

## Baking setup

- Total flour target: 750 g
- Oven style: electric fan-forced oven
- Default shelf: middle shelf
- Vessel or surface: preheated baking tray
- Preheat: 240 C for 45 minutes
- Bake temperatures: 230 C, then 210 C
- Steam: optional and modest only

## Result targets

- Crumb: tight to moderately close, not fluffy or modern-artisan open
- Crust: firm and well coloured
- Flavor: wheat-forward, slightly rustic, and suitable for tearing into
  segments
- Shape: thick round loaf with clear wedge divisions

## Variables to watch

- Whether 53% hydration is enough for the specific flour on hand
- Whether the wholemeal portion should be reduced for a cleaner shape
- Whether the radial cuts need to be deeper still
- Whether a slightly shorter proof gives a stronger archaeological silhouette
- Whether an all-white wheat version behaves more convincingly than the
  mixed-wheat stand-in

## Historical serving note

- This loaf makes the most sense as torn or broken into portions rather than
  treated like a modern sandwich loaf.
- It should pair well with oil, olives, cheese, soups, pulses, or relishes such
  as moretum.
